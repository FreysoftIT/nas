The door was pine, and it had never been meant to hold anything. It had a
brass thumb-latch and a warp along the bottom edge where two winters of damp
had got into it, and if Tal had put his shoulder to it properly at any point in
the last three hours it would have come off its hinges and taken her with it.

He had not put his shoulder to it. That was the part she kept coming back to.

Wren had her heels braced against the ridge where the floorboards changed
direction at the landing, and her back flat to the pine, and her hands flat
either side of her hips, and she had been in that position long enough that the
strain had stopped being in her arms and moved into her jaw. Every few minutes
her left heel slipped a quarter-inch and she reset it. Every time she reset it
she was deciding again. Nobody had told her that part. Oris had said *hold* as
though holding were a single act, one you performed and then had performed, and
not this — a thing you had to keep choosing at four-second intervals for three
hours in the dark.

On the other side of the door, Tal screamed.

It had a shape to it by now. It went up and stayed up and then broke and came
back, and in the gaps she could hear him breathing, and the breathing was
worse. She had counted eleven of the gaps. Somewhere around the sixth she had
started saying the words under her breath, not because she believed they helped
but because her mouth wanted something to do that was not answering him.

*One who has changed cannot cross a threshold they were not admitted through.*

It came out flat, in Oris's cadence, with Oris's small pause before *admitted*.
She had learned it at nine. She had said it aloud to a room of them at eleven
and been given bread and honey for it afterward, which she remembered better
than the saying.

*One who has changed cannot cross a threshold they were not admitted through.*

The screaming stopped.

Not the way it had stopped eleven times before — not broken off and gathering.
It stopped the way a person stops when they have finished. She felt the silence
come through the wood into her back before she understood it.

"Wren."

Her heels did not move. She would think about that later, and be unable to
decide whether it was the training or the fear, and never get further than that.

"Wren, I know you're there. I can hear you saying it."

It was his voice. Not a thing wearing it — his, with the little collapse on the
second syllable of her name that he had had since he was small, that she would
have known in a crowd, in another language, underwater. He sounded tired. He
sounded like someone who had been shouting for three hours and had thought
better of it.

"You've been saying it for about an hour," he said. "You get quieter on the
second half."

She did not answer. The rule did not say anything about answering. She had gone
looking for the part of it that covered this, in the long silence after he said
her name, and had found that the rule was eleven words long and covered almost
nothing.

"Wren." Very gently. "Why can't I come through the door?"

And her mouth — because it had been doing this all night, because it had been
given something to do at nine years old and had never been given anything else —
said, "One who has changed cannot cross a threshold they were not admitted
through."

The pause on the other side went on a long time.

"That's Oris," he said. "That's Oris's voice. You're doing her voice."

She hadn't known she was.

"Is that all of it?" He wasn't shouting. That was the thing she would keep. He
was on the floor by now, she thought, from where the voice was coming, sitting
with his back to the pine the way she had hers, so that there were two of them
and one door and about an inch and a half of wood. "That's the whole of it? That
one line?"

"Yes."

"Who told her?"

Wren opened her mouth and nothing came.

She had been in that house eleven years. She had held four doors before this
one, none of them past twenty minutes, all of them for people she did not know,
and she had said the line each time and it had worked each time, and she had
never once in eleven years arrived at the end of it and found that there was
nothing behind it. It had a founding. Everything had a founding. Somebody had
been first. Somebody had stood where she was standing with their heels against
a board and had learned something bad enough to make a rule out of, and that
person had told someone, and that someone had told someone, and it had come down
two hundred years and arrived in the mouth of a nine-year-old girl with bread
and honey afterward, and at no point in the eleven years since had she asked
what the first one saw.

"Wren?"

"I don't know," she said.

She kept holding the door. That was the thing she could still do, so she did it,
and her heel slipped its quarter-inch and she reset it, and it was four
twenty-something in the morning, and on the other side of an inch and a half of
warped pine her brother said her name again, quietly, the way you say a name
when you are not asking for anything.

She held the door until it was light.

She was still holding it when she understood that she was going to have to find
out who told her.
