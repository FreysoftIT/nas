The booking room was smaller than he'd pictured it, which he decided was a good
sign. Rooms where money actually moved were always smaller than the rooms where
people talked about money moving.

The booker was a tired woman in her fifties called Ives who had said four words
since he sat down and had not written anything. On the table between them was a
single sheet, which he had also not pictured — he had assumed a stack, and a
lawyer, and the whole architecture of a thing being made difficult on purpose.
One sheet. That was how you knew.

"Read it properly," Ives said. "People skim it and then they're surprised."

He read it properly.

League-grade meant three things and they were all on the page. It meant a
standing book rather than a contract per job, which was the part everyone wanted.
It meant his name on the ledger instead of a broker's, which was the part he
wanted. And it meant a **committed throughput** — a floor, not a ceiling. Twelve
qualifying operations a year, minimum, or the book lapsed and did not reopen.

Twelve. He'd done nine last year and thought of it as a heavy year.

"Most of it's the same work," Ives said, watching him get there. "It's the
cadence people can't hold."

"And the cadence is the point."

"The cadence is the *product*." She turned the sheet a few degrees toward him,
the way you do with someone you've decided to be honest with. "We book books, not
fixers. A book that runs twelve is worth ten times a book that runs nine, and it
isn't close, because the people buying from us are buying *predictability*.
That's the whole business. You're not being asked to be better. You're being
asked to be regular."

He heard the shape of it immediately, because it was not complicated. Twelve a
year meant one a month with room for two bad months. One a month meant a runner
who could go every month. He had four people he'd use and exactly one of them
could hold that line, and she'd held it for six years.

There was a clause about it, near the bottom, in the same size type as everything
else. *Operational personnel are the counterparty's sole responsibility,
including continuity of availability.*

Continuity of availability. He read the sentence twice, understood it completely,
and did not stop on it — the way you don't stop on a step you've climbed a
thousand times.

"Anything you want changed?" Ives said.

"The notice period on lapse."

"Everyone says the notice period." She almost smiled. "Fine. Sixty days."

The door opened.

Kes had a way of coming through a doorway that took up no room at all, which was
the single most useful thing about her and had taken years. She stopped just
inside it. She had her jacket on, which meant she'd come from somewhere rather
than been waiting, and she was standing with her weight even, which she never
did.

"Marek."

He had the sheet in his left hand and Ives's pen in his right and about four
seconds of a sixty-day notice period sitting unsecured in his head.

"One more," he said, to the sheet.

He meant the clause. One more line and it was done and she could have the rest of
the afternoon, the week, whatever she wanted. It did not occur to him that the
sentence had a second meaning, because it had never meant the second thing when
he said it, and he had said it a great many times.

"It's—"

"One more."

Some quantity of silence happened. Then the sound of a jacket moving, and the
door, and the room had two people in it again.

Ives did not look at the door. "Sixty days, then. Anything else?"

"No," he said, and signed it.

That was it. There was no moment. He had spent eleven years imagining a moment
and what there was instead was a woman capping a pen and saying *welcome to the
book* in the tone of someone confirming a delivery slot, and a single sheet going
into a folder, and the small sound of a chair.

Outside, the corridor was the same corridor. He stood in it for a second and did
the thing he had promised himself he would do, which was to notice that it had
happened. *I am counted.* He turned it over and it was true and it was his and
nobody could take it off him.

Underneath it, very quiet, something was not right. He checked it the way you
check a wound — *am I bleeding, no, then later* — and found nothing he could name,
and filed it as nerves, which is what a man files a thing under when he has no
category for it and has just gotten everything he wanted.

He went to find Kes. She'd gone.

He'd catch her tomorrow.
