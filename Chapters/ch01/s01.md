The underpass had two exits and Marek had been watching the wrong one for
eleven minutes, which he knew, and was doing on purpose.

"East is still clear," Kes said in his ear.

"I know."

"You're looking at west."

"I know."

She let it go, which was the difference between her and everyone else he'd
worked with. Somebody else would have needed telling that the east exit stops
being an exit the moment you look like a man who cares which exit he's near.

At 01:52 the courier came down the west ramp four minutes early, which is what
early people do, and Marek pushed off the wall and walked past him at a distance
of a metre and a half without either of them acknowledging the other, and the
handoff happened in the gap between two steps of a walk neither man broke.
Twenty years and it was still the cleanest thing he knew how to do.

"Got it."

"Out," Kes said. "There's a second body on the east ramp."

He was already turning. "Since when?"

"Ninety seconds. Came down slow." A pause of about one breath. "He's not looking
for you, he's *waiting*. Someone told him where to stand."

Which meant the courier had been followed, or sold, and either way the parcel in
Marek's coat had a clock on it now. He got eight metres before the man on the
east ramp made him — he felt it happen, the specific change in the air when
someone stops scanning and starts watching — and he had about two seconds to
choose between the west ramp he'd already used and the service stair he'd walked
past four times that evening because it looked like it went nowhere.

He went nowhere.

The stair came out in a loading bay behind the noodle place, which he knew
because Kes had walked the whole block twice that afternoon without being asked
to, and had mentioned it in one line, and he had heard it.

She was in the bay with the engine running.

"You saw him first," she said, when they were three streets clear.

"I saw the ramp first. You saw him."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," he agreed. "It's better."

Delacroix took delivery in the back of a bar that had never served anyone a
drink, counted nothing, and paid them in full and without argument, which was
how you knew he'd had a bad night too.

"Clean," he said.

"There was a watcher on the east ramp."

"There was." Delacroix didn't look up. "That's my problem, not yours. You got it
out."

"Whose book does this go under?"

Now Delacroix looked up. He had a face built for delivering exactly this
information to exactly this kind of man, and he did it without unkindness, which
made it worse.

"Torrance."

"Torrance was in bed."

"Torrance is on the book." He shrugged, one shoulder. "You're not being paid
less, Salt. You're being paid the same and Torrance is being *counted*. That's
the whole difference and it's the only difference and you've known it for a
decade."

In the car Kes drove and Marek watched the city do its four-in-the-morning thing
and thought about the word *counted*.

It was not complicated, the league. Everyone on the street could tell you what it
was, and everyone got a detail wrong. They booked books, not fixers — that part
everybody knew. They wanted twelve a year, or ten, or "a lot", depending on who
you asked. They paid the same as anyone and were worth four times as much,
because a name on the book was a name that got asked first, and getting asked
first is the only thing in this business that compounds.

Twenty years. He had done work that men on the book would have needed two people
and a week for, and he had done it alone, and it had gone under Torrance and
under nine other names before Torrance, and every single one of those nights he
had been paid exactly what he was owed.

"Say it," Kes said, without looking over.

"Say what?"

"You've been saying it for an hour in your face. Say it out loud, it's cheaper."

He watched a light change.

"I want to be counted," he said.

She didn't answer. He assumed, at the time, that this was because there was
nothing to say to it.
