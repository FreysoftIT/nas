The lockup had a bad door and better light than anywhere he could afford to
rent, and for about fourteen months it was where he spent every evening he
wasn't working.

She was twenty-two and quick in the way that gets people killed. That was the
first thing he'd told her and the only thing she'd argued with. So he'd stopped
arguing and started showing her instead — the difference between a route and a
route you could leave, why the third question you ask is the one that matters,
how to read a room's exits before its people. He was not a natural teacher. He
was a patient one, which turned out to be the same thing at half the speed.

Tonight she'd walked a mock corridor eleven times and gotten it wrong eleven
times, and on the twelfth she'd gotten it right without being told what she'd
changed, which is the only kind of right that sticks.

"Again," she said.

"No."

"I've got it."

"You've got it *now*." He put the bag down. "Sit."

She sat on the crate, hands loose, and he could see her running it again behind
her eyes, and it was the best thing he'd seen in a year.

He took the case out of the bag and put it on the crate beside her. Second-hand
and eight years old, and he'd spent four months' margin on it, and he'd had a
man he trusted go over it twice.

She didn't open it. She looked at it and then at him. "Marek."

"It's not a present. It's equipment." He heard himself and softened it. "It was
going to be a present. Then I priced it and decided to keep my dignity."

She opened it. She was very careful with the catches, which he noticed and
liked.

"This is—"

"It's what I didn't have." He sat down on the step opposite. "When I was where
you are, there was nobody doing this. I worked for a man who was happy to let me
learn on jobs, which is a very cheap way of teaching somebody, and it cost me
two years and a finger I still can't feel properly. So." He nodded at the case.
"Don't learn on jobs."

She looked at it for a long moment. "Why?"

"Because it's stupid and slow and people die of it."

"No — why *me*."

And this was the part he had thought about, in the way you think about a thing
you intend to say well, and he said it well, because he'd meant it for months.

"Because you're going to be better than me." He said it flatly, the way you
state a fact you've already checked. "Not in ten years. In three. You read faster
than I do and you don't get attached to a plan, and those are the two things I've
never been able to teach anybody and you had both before you walked in here.
You're going to be able to hold a line I can't hold. You'll take work I have to
turn down. In five years there's going to be a category of job that only exists
because you can do it, and people are going to have to invent a word for what you
are."

He had never said any of it out loud before and it came out clean and true and
he was proud of every syllable.

She sat with the case open on her knees.

"That's—" she said, and stopped.

He waited. She was looking at the case.

"Thank you," she said.

She sat with it a while after that and didn't say much, and he put it down to the
hour and the twelve corridors, because she'd been going since six and anybody
would be flat. He walked her to the road. She was quiet in a way he'd have
noticed on someone he knew less well, and he did notice, and he filed it under
*long day*, which is where he filed most things that year and most of them
belonged there.

At the corner she said, "Same time Thursday?"

"Same time Thursday."

He went home pleased with himself, and he had earned it, and that is the part
that would be unbearable later: **he had earned it.**
