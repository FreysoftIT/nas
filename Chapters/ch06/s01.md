The numbers were fine. That was the thing that had been bothering him for a week
and he'd only just found the shape of it: the numbers were *fine*, and they had
never been fine before. Kes overran. Kes came back with more than the brief and
an argument about the brief. For six years the reports had been a nuisance to
read and for the last two months they had been correct.

He sat with that on a Tuesday evening and did not like it.

So he did the thing he had not done in three years, which was to move a call.
Then he moved a second one. Then he put a line through the whole evening, which
cost him a conversation with the Marrow he'd been building for a month, and told
the desk he wasn't in.

When she came up he had the office lights on the low setting and nothing on the
table, and he saw her clock both of those things on the way to the chair.

"That bad?" she said.

"No. Sit down."

She sat. She looked well. She had looked well for two months, which was another
thing, now that he was counting.

"I've been reading your reports," he said.

"They're on time."

"They're perfect. You've filed nine perfect reports and you've never filed a
perfect report in your life." He leaned back. "So I'm asking. And I'm asking
properly, and I've got the whole evening, and there's nothing on this table."

She didn't say anything.

"Kes." He said it as gently as he knew how. "What do you want?"

He watched her take the question. There was a moment where something moved
behind her face and he thought, *good, we're getting to it*, and felt the
particular relief of a man who has correctly diagnosed a problem in time.

"Say that again," she said.

"What do you want?"

"No — the first part. About the reports."

"You've filed nine perfect reports."

"Nine." She nodded slowly, like a woman checking an arithmetic she'd already
done. "Right."

"Kes—"

"You want to know what I want." She said it without heat. "Now. This week."

"I want to know what's wrong."

"Those aren't the same question, but all right." She was quiet a second. Then she
smiled, and it was a good smile, and he had known her six years and did not
notice one thing about it. "I'm tired, Marek. That's all it is. Six years is a
long time to be on and I've been on. I'd like a lighter quarter and I'd like the
Kestrel corridor because it's boring and it pays, and after that I'll be fine."

It was such a reasonable answer. It was the answer of a professional who knew her
own condition and had costed the fix, which is exactly what he had spent four
years teaching her to be.

"Done," he said. "Both. Take the corridor, take the quarter."

"Thanks."

"And you tell me. Next time. Before it's nine reports."

"Yeah," she said.

He walked her down. At the door he said something about Thursday and she said
something back, and he went upstairs feeling better than he had all week —
lighter, actually, the way you do when a thing you were dreading turns out to be
a conversation.

He had asked her what she wanted. He had cleared an evening to do it. He would
have said, if anyone had asked him, that it was one of the better nights of his
year.

It was the last time he saw her with her own face on.
