"Your girl's been shopping."

Delacroix said it the way he said everything, which was as though he'd been
asked. Marek had come in for twenty minutes about a corridor schedule and they
were four minutes past the end of that conversation, in the part where Delacroix
told you things you hadn't paid for.

"Meaning."

"Meaning she's asked two people about buyout terms." He held up a hand before
Marek could get there. "Not about leaving you. About what it *costs* to stop.
Whether there's a number. Whether anyone's ever done it at her grade and had it
stick."

"Who'd she ask?"

"Wrong question."

"Who."

"Fenn, and a woman at the Marrow whose name I'm not going to say in here."
Delacroix finally looked at him. "The right question is why she asked them and
not you."

Marek sat with that for a second and found, to his surprise, that the thing
arriving in his chest was not grief.

It was *embarrassment*. She had gone to Fenn — Fenn, who couldn't run a two-man
job without a diagram — and asked him a question about her own career, in a room
Marek wasn't in, and now Delacroix knew, and if Delacroix knew then six people
knew, and every one of them had spent the last week understanding something about
Marek's operation before Marek did.

He noticed the shape of what he was feeling. He did not examine it. He put it
down and picked up the part he could work with.

*Under scrutiny, take the harder contract.*

"How long?"

"Three weeks. Maybe five."

Three weeks. So it predated the book — no, it didn't, it was *inside* the book,
it had started somewhere in the first month of twelve-a-year, and he could
practically date it now that he was looking, and none of that was the problem
he needed to solve tonight.

The problem he needed to solve tonight was that his only runner who could hold
the line was pricing her exit.

And that, at least, was a problem with a known shape. People asked about buyouts
for one reason and it was always the same reason underneath, whatever they said
out loud: they had done the arithmetic on the next five years and the number had
come out wrong. You did not fix that with a conversation. You fixed it by
changing the arithmetic.

He walked home doing the sums and had them done before he was halfway.

He gave her a third of his own cut. Not a bonus — a **restructure**, standing,
written, hers whether she ran or not, backdated one quarter. It cost him more
than the Marrow relationship he'd killed the month before and he did not have to
think about whether to do it. He wrote it on a Thursday and had it witnessed,
because a thing witnessed is a thing that survives him, and that mattered to him
in a way he didn't interrogate.

She read it twice. He watched her read it twice.

"Marek—"

"It's not a raise. It's yours. It doesn't move if you have a bad quarter and it
doesn't move if I have one." He'd rehearsed that part. "You've been carrying the
book. You should own a piece of what it makes."

She was quiet for a moment.

"Thank you," she said, and folded it, and put it in an inside pocket, and did
not take it out again while he was watching.

He went to bed that night having, by any measure he had ever been taught, handled
it. He had heard about a problem four minutes after it became visible. He had
identified the driver. He had solved it at a cost to himself, permanently, in
writing, without being asked.

He never did find out what she'd actually asked Fenn.

She'd asked whether anyone had ever stopped at her grade and had it stick, and
Fenn had said *no*, and she had said *thanks*, and that was the entire
conversation, and there was no number in it anywhere.
