The floor had a way of congratulating you that made you check your pockets
afterward.

Marek had been on it eleven years and had never once been the reason it was
loud. Now people he had done work for at half-rate were crossing the room to
put a hand on his arm and say his name like it had always been worth saying, and
he stood in the middle of it with a glass he wasn't drinking and made the face
of a man who had expected this. It came easily. It had come easily for twenty
years. That was the whole trick and it had never cost him anything before, so
he had no way of knowing that this was the last time it would be free.

*Under scrutiny, take the harder contract.* Somebody asked him what he'd do
first. He said something about the Kestrel corridor and watched three people
decide he was serious.

Then the room did the thing rooms do, and there was a gap in it, and Oyo was
standing in the gap.

He was not hostile. Marek had built a whole afternoon on the assumption that he
would be, and Oyo came through the gap with his hands where you could see them
and said, "Salt," which was the handle, and not the name, and had been the
correct address between them for a decade.

"Oyo."

"It's a good floor tonight." He looked around it like a man pricing a room.
"They'll be like this for about six weeks."

"And then?"

"Then you'll have done something." He said it without any edge at all, and that
was when Marek understood the afternoon was going to cost him something. Contempt
he could have used. Contempt in front of forty people was a gift. This was a man
being *accurate*, and accuracy from Oyo had always been the expensive kind.

"Say it, then."

"There's a paper on you." Oyo turned the glass in his hand a quarter-turn and
did not drink from it. "Nineteen years old. The Hollis job — the one before the
one you tell people about. You came out of it clean because someone stood a
guarantee for you with the Marrow, and the guarantee never got discharged, it
got *held*. It's been held ever since. You've been walking around owned since
you were twenty-six and nobody ever needed to mention it, because nobody ever
needed anything from you."

Marek did not say *that's not true*, because he had run enough of these to know
what a man looked like when he was reciting something he'd read rather than
something he'd invented. Oyo had a date. Oyo had the name of the counterparty.
The floor went on being loud around them and Marek stood inside a fact that had
been sitting under his whole career like a stone under a carpet.

"And now somebody needs something," he said.

"Now you're league." Oyo let that sit. "A paper on a street fixer is worth what
the fixer's worth. A paper on a league fixer is worth what the *league* is
worth. Somebody was going to move on it inside the month. Somebody with more
patience than sense."

"Was."

"I bought it this morning."

There it was. Marek felt the floor tilt about a degree and had the presence of
mind to keep the glass level, and something in him — the part that had been
doing this since he was nineteen — went very quiet and very fast through the
shape of it. *He owns the paper. He owns me. He waited until the exact hour it
became worth the most and he took it, in front of a room, and told me himself so
I'd know he could have done it quietly.*

It was such a clean read. It was the read anybody on that floor would have made.
It accounted for every visible fact and it was going to be wrong for eleven days.

"How much did it cost you?"

"More than it's worth."

"Then you overpaid."

"Yes," Oyo said, and for a quarter of a second he looked at the glass instead of
at Marek, and then he didn't. "I did."

Ask him why. Twenty years of doing this and every one of them said the same
thing: *when a man volunteers that he overpaid, ask him why, because the answer
is the whole shape of him.* The question was in Marek's mouth and it did not
come out, because asking it in that room, in front of that floor, on the night
he had finally arrived, would have meant standing in public as a man who did not
understand his own situation.

So he said, "Thank you." He meant it to land as *I know what you did.* It came
out as what it was, which was thanks, and the two men and about four people
within earshot all heard it land that way.

Oyo took it without expression. "Congratulations on the league, Salt," he said,
and went back into the room, and the floor closed over the gap where he had been
like water.
