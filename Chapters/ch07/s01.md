Four rounds. Two low, one through the shoulder, one he could not account for,
and the fact that he was counting at all was going to be the thing people
repeated afterward, if there was an afterward.

He was on his side against the kerb with his cheek in the wet and the street
laid out in front of him at a bad angle, and what he was doing — what he could
not stop doing — was reading it.

*Assess the street.* He had taught her that, six years ago, somewhere cold, with
his hand flat on a rail: four questions in a fixed order, and the order mattered
more than the answers.

No overwatch on the roofline.

No second vehicle.

The shutter across the way had come down at some point and had not gone back up,
which meant somebody had watched and decided not to.

Twelve minutes to the perimeter at a run, and nobody was running.

Four questions, four answers, and what he had never once examined in twenty years
of teaching that order was that it put your own body last. His body was one item
on a list and it was not the first item. He had built the habit so well that he
had to lie in the wet and wait his turn.

Nobody said *east is still clear.* The quiet had a particular quality to it — the
quality of a street with no one in your ear.

Then the cold came up through his side — not pain, the cold, a slow flood
arriving from under his ribs and moving with purpose — and it moved the list
around.

He knew who.

That was the part he had been carrying since the second round went in, carrying
the way you carry something you have not put down and have not looked at either.
He had seen the shape at the mouth of the alley and had known it the way you know
a walk — before the face, before the light, in the shoulders, six years of
watching that walk come toward him across every bad room in this city — and there
had been about a half-second in which the whole of him had gone *no, she's east
tonight,* and in the same half-second, underneath it, *good, she's here.*

Then the third round.

Not betrayal. That was the wrong word and he had time to be precise about it,
lying there, because precision was the last of him that still worked.
Betrayal was a thing you did to someone you owed. He had been standing in the
open, in the light where she left him — standing in it, because he had stopped
checking rooflines himself somewhere around the year he started sending other
people to check them — and she had done exactly what the situation called for.

*I saw the ramp first. You saw him.*

If he had been running it he would have called it the same way. He would have
called it clean.

Hands on him.

He came back and there were hands on him, someone kneeling with a knee in the wet
and both palms pressed flat over the shoulder wound, hard enough to register
through the cold, and Marek looked up past the wrists — big hands, scarred across
the knuckles, hands that had turned a glass a quarter-turn while they told him
what he was worth — and it was Oyo.

Of course it was.

"Don't," Oyo said. Not to him — to the wound, or to the situation, or to the
general arrangement of the evening. His face was doing nothing at all. "Don't
talk."

Marek talked. What came out was: "Who's on the roofline?"

Because that was the question. That was the *operational* question, and Oyo, who
had spent eleven years being the man Marek measured himself against, did not
laugh at it and did not answer it either. He shifted his weight, got more of
himself over the pressure, and said, "Nobody. I looked."

*I looked.*

Which meant he had come in checking angles for a man he had wanted to lose for a
decade, had found none, and had knelt down in the open anyway, in a street with
an unaccounted-for fourth round still in it.

And Marek — dying, and still a fixer, the two turning out not to interfere with
each other at all — put it where it went. *He owns the paper. He owns me.* You
did not let an asset bleed out in the road. That was the arithmetic, and it was
clean, and it meant he did not have to feel anything about it.

He believed that for about two seconds.

Then he looked at where the hands actually were — flat, and hard, and shaking
slightly, and not on the low wounds, which were the ones killing him, but on the
one Oyo could reach — and understood that a man protecting an asset does not
kneel in an open street.

A man protecting an asset makes a call.

He could not do anything with that. It went on a list.

And then, because the cold had moved things around and the ordering had gone
soft, he was somewhere else. Three weeks back. The booking room, which had been
smaller than he'd pictured it. Ives capping a pen. Kes in the door with her
jacket already on and that particular stillness she got when she had decided
something and was giving you one chance to make it unnecessary.

A sheet in one hand. Sixty days of notice period sitting unsecured in his head.

He had said, "One more."

He had meant the clause. He was certain of that, then and now — one more line and
it was done and she could have the whole afternoon. He had meant a clause on a
page.

She had said, *It's—*

And he had said it again.

And she had gone.

He did not know the end of her sentence. That was the thing. That was the whole
of it, and it had taken him three weeks and four rounds to arrive at it: he had
cut a sentence in half in a doorway and then walked around for twenty-two days
believing he had had a conversation.

He could infer it. He was good at inferring. She was in year seven and there was
no year eight; the number that made the league work was the same number that made
her disposable; whoever picked up her book would run her at that rate until she
stopped, and she knew it, and she had come to the door to say so.

That was probably it.

He would never know if it was.

*Never sends a runner somewhere he wouldn't go.*

He had said that about himself for twenty years and believed it while he was
saying it. And it was not that he had broken it tonight, or three weeks ago, or
on any nameable day — it was that he could not now find the year in which it had
last been true, and what went through him worse than the shoulder was that he had
never had to break it deliberately. It had gone
soft under him one *one more* at a time. Nobody had ever stood in a door and
asked him for the second half of the sentence.

She had. Three weeks ago. That was what the stillness was.

"Oyo," he said.

"Shut up."

He had it. For about four seconds he had all of it, the whole shape, and what he
needed to say was *find her — she did the correct thing and correct is what I
taught her and she has nowhere to be after tonight* — and then, after that, the
part that mattered: *don't buy her book. Whatever it costs. Let it lapse.*

He had it. In order. Four questions, with his own body last.

"Tell her it's not on her," he said.

Wrong one.

He heard it come out and knew it was the wrong one — not untrue, just useless, a
thing that would comfort exactly one person and it was not her. The last sentence
he would get, and he had spent it on himself, which was at least consistent.

Oyo did not ask who.

He leaned his weight down and said, "You can tell her yourself," in a voice like
a door closing, and above them the shutter across the way went up about a foot
and stopped, and stayed like that, and nobody came out.

And underneath them, the real defect: **`setup_one_more` was declared planted in
ch03 b3, `SETUP-1` reported it ✅ "Planted at b3", and the words appeared nowhere
in ch03's prose.** Likewise `"I'm past the rate"` existed in exactly one place in

were declared in metadata and absent from the text.**

Every reconciliation in NAS folds *deltas*, and deltas do not carry dialogue or
staging. The two scenes emitted perfectly consistent deltas. Their prose
contradicted. Nothing was capable of noticing.

*Repair, in the direction of the canonical render:*

1. **ch03 now plants it for real** — he says `"One more,"` twice, meaning *one
   more clause*, and the prose says so. The setup exists in the artifact.
2. **ch07's recollection is corrected** to the booking room and to the words
   actually spoken.
3. **Her line is not restored, because she never got to finish it.** He cut her
   off. So the reveal at b4 is no longer Marek quoting her — it is Marek
   realising *he does not know the end of her sentence* and inferring it. This is
   strictly better: it converts a continuity bug into the scene's sharpest beat,
   and it is why `"Tell her it's not on her"` is the wrong line. He cannot answer
   a question he never heard.

The divergence that remains is now **authored**: he meant a clause, she heard a
job, and §7's two-place modality already models exactly that without a special
case. What was a contradiction is now a read.
