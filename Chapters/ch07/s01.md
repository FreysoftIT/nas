Four rounds. Two low, one through the shoulder, one he could not account for,
and the fact that he was counting at all was going to be the thing people
repeated afterward, if there was an afterward.

He was on his side against the kerb with his cheek in the wet and the street
laid out in front of him at a bad angle, and what he was doing — what he could
not stop doing — was reading it. No overwatch on the roofline. No second vehicle.
The shutter across the way had come down at some point and had not gone back up,
which meant somebody had watched and decided not to. Twelve minutes to the
perimeter at a run and there was nobody running.

*Assess the street.* Twenty years of it. His body was one item on a list and it
was not the first item.

Then the cold came up through his side, and it moved the list around.

He knew who. That was the part he had been carrying since the second round went
in, carrying the way you carry something you have not put down and have not
looked at either. He had seen the shape at the mouth of the alley and had known
it the way you know a walk — before the face, before the light, in the shoulders
— and there had been about a half-second where the whole of him had gone *no,
she's on the east side tonight*, and then the third round.

Not betrayal. That was the wrong word and he had time to be precise about it,
lying there, because precision was all he had left that still worked. Betrayal
was a thing you did to someone you owed. He had been standing in the light where
she left him and she had done exactly what the situation called for, and if he
had been running it he would have called it the same way.

Hands on him.

He came back and there were hands on him, someone kneeling with a knee in the
wet and both palms pressed flat over the shoulder wound, and Marek looked up
past the wrists and it was Oyo.

Of course it was.

"Don't," Oyo said. Not to him — to the wound, or to the situation. His face was
doing nothing at all. "Don't talk."

Marek talked. What came out was: "Who's on the roofline?"

Because that was the question. That was the *operational* question, and Oyo,
who had spent eleven years being the man Marek measured himself against, did
not laugh at it and did not answer it either. He shifted his weight, got more
of himself over the pressure, and said, "Nobody. I looked."

*I looked.* Which meant he had come in checking angles for a man he had wanted
to lose for a decade, and had found none, and had knelt down in the open anyway.
Marek registered that and could not do anything with it. It went on a list.

And then, because the cold had moved things around and the ordering had gone
soft, he was somewhere else — three weeks back, the office, Kes standing at the
door with her jacket already on and that particular stillness she got when she
had decided something and was giving you one chance to make it unnecessary. She
had said: *I'm past the rate.* And he had been looking at a schedule.

He had said, "One more."

Not cruelly. Not even paying attention. The way you say *mind the step*. Two
words, dropped into the door of his own office at a woman he had built out of
nothing over six years, and he had not looked up, and he had meant *one more and
then we'll talk*, and he had never once in his life said the second half of that
sentence to anyone.

*Never sends a runner somewhere he wouldn't go.*

He had said it to himself for twenty years. He had believed it while he was
saying it. And it was not that he had broken it tonight, or last week — it was
that he could not now find the year in which it had last been true, and the
awful thing, the thing that went through him worse than the shoulder, was that
he had never had to break it deliberately. It had gone soft under him one *one
more* at a time, and nobody had ever stood in the door and asked him for the
second half.

She had. Three weeks ago. That was what that stillness was.

"Oyo," he said.

"Shut up."

He had it. For about four seconds he had all of it, the whole shape, and what he
needed to say was *she's past the rate, she's in year seven, whoever picks up
her contract will run her till she's dead and she knows it and that's why*, and
what he needed to say after that was *go and find her*, and he had it, he had it
right there.

"Tell her it's not on her," he said.

Wrong one. He heard it come out and knew it was the wrong one — not untrue, just
useless, a thing that would comfort exactly one person and it was not her.

Oyo did not ask who. He leaned his weight down and said, "You can tell her
yourself," in a voice like a door closing, and above them the shutter across the
way went up about a foot and stopped, and stayed like that, and nobody came out.
