Oris was cutting bread when Wren came in, which was the first wrong thing.

Not wrong. Ordinary. Oris had two rooms above the yard and she had been in them
for as long as Wren had known her, and at half past seven in the morning she was
standing at the board by the window with a knife and a loaf and the light coming
in flat across both, and there was no reason on earth for her to be doing
anything else. Wren had walked here from a door she had held for six hours with
her brother's voice on the other side of it, and Oris was cutting bread, and the
distance between those two facts was so wide that Wren stopped in the doorway and
could not think of the first word.

"Sit," Oris said, without turning. "You look like a dropped plate."

She sat. Her hands had started shaking somewhere on the stairs and had not
stopped, and she put them under the table.

Oris brought the bread over and put honey on it and set it down in front of her,
and that was the second wrong thing, because it was exactly what she had done
when Wren was nine and had said the words aloud to a room of them and got them
right. The same plate, probably. Wren looked at it and did not touch it, and Oris
sat down across from her and waited with the particular stillness of a woman who
had waited out worse than this and knew that it worked.

Wren did not ask.

She had come here to ask. She had walked the whole way rehearsing it, and she had
got as far as the stairs still holding it, and now she was sitting in front of
bread and honey and what came out of her mouth was, "It held."

"I know."

"Six hours."

"I know that too." Oris did not move. "You didn't come here to tell me the door
held, Wren."

The room was very quiet. Below them somebody crossed the yard with a bucket.

"Who told you?"

Oris's face did not change, and afterward that was the part Wren kept — that it
did not change, that there was no flicker of a thing being hidden, no pause where
a lie assembles itself. Oris looked at her the way you look at someone who has
asked what day it is.

"Bettan," she said.

"And who told Bettan?"

"I don't know."

Wren waited. She waited long enough that the waiting became its own question, and
Oris let it, and then Oris said, mildly, as though it had only just occurred to
her that it might need saying at all:

"I never asked her."

There it was. Wren heard it land somewhere under her ribs and go on falling.

"You never—"

"It wasn't a thing you asked." Oris turned the plate a quarter-turn, so the honey
side faced Wren, an old gesture, meaningless. "She taught it to me in the yard
when I was eleven, with her hand on the frame so I'd feel where the wood gives.
I taught it to you in that yard. She had it from someone, and that someone had it
from someone, and at no point in any of it did anybody stand where you're
standing and say *who told you*." She said it without weight. It was not a
confession. She was answering a question about the weather. "You're the first
one, that I know of. That's not nothing, but it isn't what you came for either."

Wren sat with her hands under the table.

It would have been better if Oris had lied. A lie meant there was something to
lie about — a thing known and kept, a door with someone behind it. What she had
instead was a line eleven words long that had been walked forward, hand to hand,
for two hundred years by people who were all exactly as certain as she was and
none of whom had ever turned around.

Nobody was hiding it. That was the whole of it. There was nothing to hide and
nobody to hide it, and she had spent six hours in the dark with her heels braced
and her brother saying her name, running on a sentence that no living person
could source.

"Wren." Oris's voice, and something in it that had not been there before —
not alarm. Attention. "What happened to the door?"

Wren looked up.

"It held," she said again, and heard how it sounded, and understood that she was
going to have to find Bettan, and that Bettan had been dead for nine years.
